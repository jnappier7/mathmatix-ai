Why a triangle holds and a square does not

Look at a roof truss, a bridge, a bike frame, a crane. They are built out of triangles, and not for decoration. Pull on the two shapes below and you will see the reason — then find out exactly how much you need to know about a triangle before it is pinned down for good.

Station 1

Pin it and pull

Both shapes are bars joined by pins. The bars cannot stretch, bend or shorten — the numbers on them never change. One corner is pinned to the table (the dark dot); drag any red corner.

Three bars

Drag B or C.

Four bars

Drag Q, R or S.

The brace is one more bar across the four-bar shape. It does not make the bars any stronger — it cuts the shape into two triangles. That is the whole trick, and it is why you see diagonals in every scaffold and gate you walk past.

Station 2

Lock what you are given, then try to break it

Two triangles that start out identical. Padlock the parts you have actually been told — they turn black on both diagrams, with the usual tick marks and arcs — and then push the blue triangle around using everything you did not lock. If you can make it a different shape, those parts were never going to prove anything.

Station 3

How much do you have to know?

A triangle has six parts: three sides and three angles. You rarely get told all six. Pick a set of parts below, then move the sliders and watch how many different triangles can be built from them. Freeze a part with the padlock to hold it still while you change the others.

Every number in that table is measured off the drawing, not copied from the sliders.
